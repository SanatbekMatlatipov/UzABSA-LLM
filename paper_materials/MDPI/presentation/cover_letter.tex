\opening{Dear Editors,}

We submit our manuscript \textbf{``UzABSA-LLM: Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning and Multi-Domain Annotation for Uzbek Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis''} for consideration as an original research article in \textit{Big Data and Cognitive Computing}, for the Special Issue ``Natural Language Processing Applications in Big Data''.

\textbf{Significance.} The paper does two things: it establishes what parameter-efficient fine-tuning delivers for Uzbek aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA), and it turns the resulting model into a pipeline application that converts 5{,}038 business reviews across 23 domains into a quality-scored annotated resource. Uzbek, a Turkic language with over 38 million speakers, is under-served by multilingual models owing to sparse pre-training representation, agglutinative morphology, and script duality. This is the first controlled comparison of QLoRA-adapted open-weight 7--8B models on the UzABSA benchmark, and the first to benchmark them against monolingual Uzbek encoders.

\textbf{Findings in context.} While generative aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) has focused on high-resource languages so far, earlier studies involving Uzbek, which is widely used in Central Asia, but remains a low-resource language in the context of manually annotated corpora, are mainly based on rule- or classical methods or use encoders. In comparison with zero-shot baselines on the same split of data, QLoRA fine-tuning results in at least doubling of extraction F1 and tripling of end-to-end pair F1. Qwen 2.5-7B provides the best extraction F1 (0.708) with 100% parse rate of JSON, while Llama 3.1-8B offers the best pair F1 (0.651) and sentiment accuracy (0.925). A paired bootstrap test shows no statistical significance between these two models, and we report our findings without declaring any winner. More directly relevant to practice, fine-tuned Uzbek encoders with 110M parameters are equal to 7-8 billion parameter models in aspect extraction but perform worse on sentiment and joint tasks, thus demonstrating a trade-off in cost-effectiveness for this language which was not established before.

\textbf{Fit to BDCC.} For user-generated text in a low-resource language, the binding constraint on large-scale analysis is not storage or compute but \emph{annotation}: a heterogeneous review stream cannot become an analytical resource without labels that do not exist and cannot be produced by hand at scale. Our pipeline performs that conversion, and we report its economics from measured runs rather than asserting scalability: roughly 840 reviews per hour on a single GPU, with automated quality scoring of the whole corpus costing under one US dollar. The remaining cost is human, which is why the automatic layers run exhaustively while scarce native-speaker effort goes to calibrating them.

That calibration is where the work meets the journal's cognitive-computing side. We substitute machine judgement for human judgement and treat the substitution as a claim to be tested: an LLM judge is calibrated against native-speaker ratings, and we report where it succeeds ($\rho=0.81$ on sentiment, $\rho=0.66$ on the tier-defining overall score) and where it does not (completeness, where it is systematically harsher than humans). The study also yields a finding about the task itself: annotators agreed on the polarity of every jointly identified aspect, whereas \emph{what counts as the aspect span} was the dominant disagreement even between trained native speakers. Human and machine annotators diverge in the same place, and this calibration is what licenses the released dataset's quality tiers.

\textbf{Prior MDPI submission.} As required, we disclose that an earlier version of this manuscript was submitted to \textit{Computers} (MDPI) under manuscript ID \textbf{computers-4419237} and was not accepted; this ID is also supplied in the submission system. The present version differs substantially: all models were retrained after we identified and corrected two defects in the earlier pipeline (a system-prompt serialization bug and text-level overlap between the training and evaluation splits), and we added zero-shot controls, encoder baselines, paired-bootstrap significance testing, and a native-speaker validation study. The results are new and the claims narrowed accordingly. The author list is unchanged from that submission.
